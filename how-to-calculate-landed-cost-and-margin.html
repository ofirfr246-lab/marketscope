Why landed cost matters more than the supplier’s price

The number on an AliExpress or Alibaba listing is not your cost. Landed cost is the full amount it takes to get one sellable unit to the place from which you can fulfill an order. If you skip freight, duties, brokerage, packaging or shrinkage, you will think the margin is healthy until the first invoice from a forwarder — or a customs entry — says otherwise.

This guide gives you the formulas, a clearly labelled illustrative example, and the official references you should use for Incoterms and U.S. duties. It does not replace a customs broker, and it does not quote a duty rate for your SKU.

The landed cost formula

A practical definition for ecommerce:

Landed cost per sellable unit = (product cost + inbound freight and insurance + import duties + brokerage and other non-recoverable fees + destination handling and packaging needed to make the unit saleable) ÷ sellable units received.

Use amounts you actually paid when you have them. During planning, use estimates and label them as estimates. When the shipment arrives, replace the estimates with actuals. Divide by sellable units, not units ordered: if 4 of 100 arrive damaged and cannot be sold, those 4 still belong in the cost of the 96 you can sell.

Outbound shipping to your customer, payment fees and ads are usually not part of landed cost. They belong in contribution margin, which we will calculate next. Mixing the two makes it hard to see whether the problem is sourcing or selling.

Start from the Incoterm, not from a vague “shipping included”

Two quotes are not comparable until they use the same cost boundary. A factory price quoted EXW and a price quoted FOB or DDP include different slices of the journey. The Incoterms® rules, published by the International Chamber of Commerce, are the accepted definitions for those commercial terms in contracts for the sale of goods. ICC first published them in 1936 and updates them periodically; the current version is Incoterms® 2020.

You do not need to memorize every rule. You do need to know, for each quote:

  • Where risk and cost transfer from the seller to you.
  • Who pays export clearance, main freight, insurance, import clearance and duties.
  • Whether the quote is for one sample, a trial carton, or a bulk order.

If a supplier will not state the Incoterm, treat the price as incomplete. For a comparison of common sourcing platforms, see AliExpress vs Alibaba vs 1688.

Duties are classified, not guessed

Duty is not “about 10%” and it is not a flat fee on every low-value packet. For imports into the United States, duty depends on the product’s classification in the Harmonized Tariff Schedule, the country of origin, and the rules in force when the entry is made. CBP explains this in Determining Duty Rates and notes that CBP, not the importer, makes the final determination. You can look up a starting point in the U.S. International Trade Commission HTS. For a binding answer on a specific item, CBP describes how to request a binding ruling.

Do not copy a duty rate from a blog post or from a competitor’s marketing page. Classification can turn on materials, construction and use. Two “looks-the-same” gadgets can fall in different lines.

Low-value shipments and de minimis (United States, 2026)

Many dropshipping models used to assume that low-value packages entered the United States duty-free under the Section 321 de minimis exemption. That assumption is no longer safe. CBP’s e-commerce FAQs state that the indefinite suspension of the de minimis exemption applies to merchandise valued at $800 or less arriving via all modes, including the international postal network, unless a separate exception applies (CBP notes that bona fide gifts and personal or household articles accompanying travelers remain unchanged). A related Federal Register notice covers the suspension for non-postal modes. Always read the current CBP page before you model a U.S. landed cost — this area has been changing.

If you sell into other countries, look up that country’s current low-value and VAT/GST import rules. Do not export a U.S. assumption into the United Kingdom, the EU, Canada or Australia.

Illustrative example (not a real product)

The figures below are a worked example so you can see the arithmetic. They are not a MarketScope report, not a quote from a supplier, and not a prediction.

Suppose you plan to sell a small home gadget in the United States at $39.00, and a trial shipment of 100 units is invoiced as follows:

Cost lineShipment totalPer unit (100 ordered)
Product (FOB, export packed)$1,200$12.00
International freight and insurance$180$1.80
Import duty and non-recoverable entry fees (illustrative)$140$1.40
Destination handling and mailer packaging$80$0.80
Total before shrinkage$1,600$16.00

If 4 units arrive unsellable, landed cost per sellable unit is $1,600 ÷ 96 = $16.67, not $16.00. That is the number that should go into your cost of goods.

Gross margin

Gross margin = (selling price − landed cost) ÷ selling price.

In the example: ($39.00 − $16.67) ÷ $39.00 = 57.3%. Gross profit per unit is $22.33. That is what remains to cover selling costs and overhead. It is not profit.

Contribution margin and break-even CPA

Now subtract the costs that move with an order. Still illustrative:

  • Outbound shipping you do not recover: $4.50
  • Payment processing (example: 2.9% + $0.30): $1.43
  • Expected refunds and reships (example: 8% of the selling price, net of a recovered unit): $3.12

Contribution before ads = $39.00 − $16.67 − $4.50 − $1.43 − $3.12 = $13.28.

If you also pay a marketplace referral fee, subtract that too. If you sell on your own site, you may have a lower fee line and a higher ad line.

Break-even CPA is that $13.28 (or whatever your sheet produces). If you pay more than that to acquire an order, you lose money on the incremental sale. A test budget should be judged against this number, not against “the ads feel cheap.”

If 40% of your visitors who buy also add a second item, you can model a higher average order value — but only after you have data. Do not invent an attachment rate to rescue a weak hero product.

What changes in dropshipping

When a supplier ships each order to your customer, you may never see a container invoice. You still have a landed cost: the all-in amount you pay the supplier for that order, plus any duties, taxes or fees billed to you or to the customer at the border, plus your packaging or inserts if you use them, plus the expected cost of failed deliveries and replacements.

Ask the supplier, in writing, who is the importer of record, what happens if a package is delayed or seized, and whether the checkout price you see is the price you will be charged on a busy week. Then place a sample order to yourself and compare the invoice with the listing.

Mistakes that hide a bad product

  • Comparing EXW to DDP quotes as if they were the same unit price.
  • Using dimensional weight for a bulky item but quoting the supplier’s “500 g” figure in the model.
  • Ignoring returns on products with sizing, odor, fragility or “too good to be true” claims.
  • Treating a first-class sample shipment as the cost of a standard customer shipment.
  • Stopping at gross margin and then wondering why a “60% margin” product loses money once ads run.

Frequently asked questions

What is landed cost in ecommerce?

It is the full cost of getting a sellable unit to the location from which you can fulfill it: product, inbound freight and insurance, duties, brokerage and destination handling, divided by sellable units received. Outbound shipping, ads and payment fees are usually tracked separately in contribution margin.

How do I calculate profit margin after landed cost?

Gross margin is (selling price − landed cost) ÷ selling price. Contribution margin then subtracts variable selling costs such as outbound shipping, fees, expected refunds and ads. Use contribution, not gross margin, to set a break-even CPA.

Does the U.S. de minimis exemption still apply to dropshipping parcels?

Do not assume that it does. CBP’s e-commerce FAQs describe an indefinite suspension of the de minimis exemption for merchandise valued at $800 or less arriving by all modes, with limited exceptions. Read the current official page before you model U.S. costs.

Should I include advertising in landed cost?

Usually no. Keep landed cost as the cost of the goods ready to sell. Put advertising in contribution margin so you can see sourcing and acquisition as two levers.
