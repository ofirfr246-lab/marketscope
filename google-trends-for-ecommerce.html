What Google Trends is — and what it is not

Google Trends is one of the few demand tools you can open for free, re-run later, and explain to a partner without a vendor login. Used well, it will tell you whether interest in a product or a problem is rising, stable, seasonal or already fading in a country you care about. Used poorly, it will convince you that a spike is a business.

Google describes Trends in two official places you should read once: the FAQ about Google Trends data and Search Central’s Get started with Google Trends. The facts that matter for ecommerce research are:

  • Trends is based on a sample of anonymized, aggregated Google searches, not on every query and not on orders, revenue or ads.
  • Numbers are normalized and then scaled to 0–100 so you can compare relative interest over the selected time and place. A 50 is not “50 searches.” Regions with the same Trends value do not necessarily have the same total search volume.
  • Low-volume terms can appear as 0, and they can also show statistical noise that is not real interest. Google warns that unusual one-off spikes on thin queries should not be read as evidence of individual searches.
  • Trends is not a poll and not the same thing as Google Ads search-volume reports.

If you need that distinction later, this page is the short version; the FAQ is the source.

Use Explore, not only “Trending now”

Trends has more than one surface. For product research, start with the Explore tool, where you choose the terms, the country, the time range and the Google property. “Trending now” is useful for watching what is moving today. It is the wrong first screen if you are trying to decide whether a product has a multi-year shape.

A repeatable setup for ecommerce:

  1. Open Explore and set the location to the country you will sell in, not “worldwide,” unless you truly ship everywhere and can fulfill everywhere.
  2. Set the range to five years first, so you can see seasonality and whether a recent rise is new or a returning peak. Then use a shorter window (for example 90 days) only to inspect recent momentum.
  3. Enter the product term and one or two alternate names or “job” phrases (what the buyer would type). Explore lets you compare a small set of terms on the same chart.
  4. Where it helps, compare a search term with a topic. Search Central notes that topics group misspellings and related queries, which is useful when a product has several names.
  5. Save the chart, the parameters, and the date. Trends results depend on the filters you chose; a screenshot without a country or range is not evidence.

How to read the shapes

Shape on a five-year chartWhat it usually suggestsWhat to do next
Stable or gently risingOngoing interest, if the term is specific enough to be meaningful.Go on to marketplace and margin checks. Demand may be real, and it may already be competitive.
Regular seasonal peaksA calendar you can plan around, not a surprise spike.Time your test, content and inventory before the peak in that country. Search Central notes that the same topic can peak at different times in different countries.
A sharp spike, then a drop that does not recoverA fad, a news event, or a one-off viral week.Do not build a store on the leftover attention. Re-check in 90 days. See TikTok made me buy it research.
A recent rise on top of a longer basePossible momentum inside a real category.Confirm with a shorter window, related queries, and shopping demand — then watch saturation.
Flat near zero with odd one-week spikesOften a thin term, or noise.Try a broader topic, a different name, or accept that Trends may not have a useful sample for this query.

Never compare two charts that were not run with the same location, time range and property. A term that “hits 100” on a 30-day chart has only hit the top of that window.

Related queries are often the real research

The related queries and related topics cards are where a vague product idea becomes a sub-niche. Search Central’s Trends guide recommends using Top to see what people already associate with the term, and Rising (including breakout queries) to see what has been gaining attention.

For ecommerce, that list is a source of:

  • Variant and attribute language (“stainless”, “for travel”, “refill”, “kids”).
  • Problem language you can use on a page, if it is accurate.
  • Adjacent products that might be a bundle — or a sign that the searcher wants something else.
  • Warning queries (complaints, scams, recalls) that you should read before you sell the item.

Not every related query is a product opportunity. Keep only the lines that match the product you can actually source and support.

Trends is one demand signal, not the whole validation

A healthy Trends line does not tell you the price, the refund rate, or how many sellers already sit on the Shopping carousel. After you like the shape:

  • Check marketplace listings and recent reviews in the same country (Amazon versus Google Shopping demand explains why those surfaces differ).
  • Look at saturation and price clustering.
  • Build a conservative landed cost and contribution margin.
  • If the idea still stands, follow the validation sequence, including a sample.

Google’s own Trends guidance for site owners is similar in spirit: do not write about something only because it is trending, and check that the topic fits the audience you actually serve.

Common mistakes in product research

  • Running a worldwide query for a store that will sell in one country.
  • Using a 7-day or 30-day window as proof of a long-term business.
  • Treating 100 as “huge demand” without a comparison term (a small niche can hit 100 against itself).
  • Comparing two products from two different screenshots taken months apart, with different filters.
  • Ignoring Google’s warning about noise on low-volume terms, and calling a jagged line a “breakout.”
  • Stopping at Trends and never opening a landed-cost sheet.

Frequently asked questions

How do I use Google Trends for ecommerce product research?

In Explore, set the country you will sell in, start with a five-year range, compare a few buyer-language terms, then read related queries. Use the shape (stable, seasonal, spike, rising) as one demand signal, and confirm it with marketplace activity and unit economics.

Does a Google Trends score of 100 mean high search volume?

No. 100 is the peak of relative interest for the terms, location and dates you selected. A small niche can reach 100 on its own chart. The official FAQ explains that results are normalized and scaled to 0–100, and that the same interest value can represent different total volumes in different regions.

Should I use a 90-day or a five-year range?

Use five years first so you can see seasonality and whether a rise is new. Use a shorter range only after that, to inspect recent momentum. Do not compare two products across two different ranges.

Can I rely on Trends alone to pick a product?

No. Trends does not measure sellers, prices, ads, landed cost or refunds. It is a demand-shape tool. A complete validation still needs those other checks.

Is MarketScope the same as Google Trends?

No. Trends is Google’s relative-interest tool. MarketScope uses Google Search and Google Shopping data in live reports to score demand, competition, saturation and opportunity for a product in a market you choose, and it does not replace a Trends seasonality check.
