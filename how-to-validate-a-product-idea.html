What “validated” should mean

A product idea is not validated because a supplier called it a “winning product,” because a video went viral, or because a tool produced a high score. Those are leads. Validation is the point at which you can defend a go, revise, or stop decision with evidence you can show someone else.

Three independent checks have to pass before a launch is justified:

  • Demand. People in your target market are already looking for the product or the job it does, and the interest is not only a one-week spike.
  • Economics. After landed cost, fees, expected refunds and a conservative ad cost, a sale still contributes cash. Volume cannot rescue a negative contribution margin.
  • Supply. You have seen the physical product, measured handling and transit time, and have a plausible backup source.

If any one of those is missing, the honest status is “not validated yet,” even if the idea is exciting. This sequence is the tighter version of how to find winning products before you spend on ads.

1. Write the offer before you open a tool

Start with a one-page brief, not a store theme:

  • The buyer and the situation (who has the problem, where they are, what they already try).
  • The product in precise language, including variants, size, materials and what is in the box.
  • The market you will sell in (country first, language second).
  • A working retail price and the shipping promise you think you can keep.
  • The claims you plan to make — and whether you can prove each one.

If you cannot fill that page, you do not yet have a product idea. You have a category. Go back to choosing a niche and narrow it.

2. Collect demand evidence from more than one place

Require at least two independent demand sources that agree. Independent means they are not just copies of the same viral moment.

Search interest

In Google Trends Explore, compare the product name and two alternate names over five years, in your country, so they share a scale. You are reading the shape: stable, rising, seasonal, or a spike that has already faded. Official Trends data is a sampled, normalized index, not order volume; see Google’s FAQ about Trends data and our guide to using Google Trends for ecommerce.

Marketplace activity

Search the same terms on the marketplaces your customers already use. Look for recent reviews, not only large lifetime totals. A listing with thousands of old reviews and almost no new ones is a different story from a listing that keeps collecting reviews this quarter. Note the price range and whether search suggestions sound like buyer language.

Ads that have been allowed to keep running

The Meta Ad Library shows active ads on Facebook and Instagram and when they started. Advertisers usually stop ads that lose money, so several unrelated accounts running the same product for weeks is a stronger signal than a single new creative. It is also a warning that customer acquisition may already be expensive. Pair this with the saturation checks in how to spot product saturation.

Weak signals, on their own: one viral video, a store’s “sold” counter, a tool’s revenue estimate, or a friend saying they would buy it.

3. Check unit economics before you buy inventory or ads

Sketch the unit on paper with conservative numbers. The full method, including duties and a worked example, is in how to calculate landed cost and margin. The short version:

  • Landed cost is what it takes to get one sellable unit ready to fulfill — product, inbound freight, duties, brokerage, packaging — not just the supplier’s headline price.
  • Gross margin uses selling price minus landed cost.
  • Contribution margin then subtracts the costs that move with each order: outbound shipping, payment and marketplace fees, expected refunds and reships, and variable ad spend.
  • Break-even CPA is the most you can pay to acquire an order before the contribution turns negative.

Model a normal case and a worse case (higher shipping, higher refunds, higher CPA). If only the optimistic case works, the idea is not validated. In the United States, also include current import treatment: CBP has documented the indefinite suspension of the de minimis exemption for low-value shipments, so “it will just arrive duty-free” is not a plan.

4. Test supply with samples, not with the supplier’s photos

Order the product you would actually sell, shipped the way a customer would receive it, from your leading supplier and from one backup. Record:

  • When the order was placed, when tracking appeared, and when it arrived.
  • Whether the item matches the listing: materials, finish, accessories, smell, noise, assembly, packaging.
  • Which claims on your draft page the sample can actually support. Delete the rest.
  • Whether a second supplier can match the same specification.

A supplier’s stated shipping time is a marketing number until you measure it. If samples took 8–12 days, do not promise “arrives in 5.” For sourcing channel differences, see AliExpress vs Alibaba vs 1688.

5. Run hard stops for compliance and IP

Some problems cannot be fixed by better ads:

  • You cannot verify safety, labeling or certification documents the destination market requires.
  • The design, character, logo or trademark appears to belong to someone else.
  • The product needs health, medical or performance claims you cannot substantiate.
  • The supplier cannot hold a stable specification from sample to production.

If a hard stop applies, stop. Popularity does not cancel a takedown, a customs seizure or a refund wave.

6. If the desk work passes, run a small, pre-committed test

Validation is not finished at the spreadsheet. A tight live test answers a different question: will your offer, page and creative convert at a CPA you can afford?

Decide the following in writing before you spend:

  • Budget cap and end date.
  • Target CPA (at or below your conservative break-even).
  • The audience, hook and offer you are actually testing — one idea at a time.
  • Stop rules: for example, stop if CPA stays above break-even after a stated number of clicks or days, or if the page has a serious conversion or policy problem.

Do not raise the budget to “give it a chance” after the stop rule hits. Revise the offer, the price, the creative or the product, then test again — or drop it and take the next candidate.

Go, revise or stop

DecisionWhen it is justified
GoAt least two demand sources agree, conservative contribution is positive, the sample passes, and no hard stop applies.
ReviseThe problem is real, but price, bundle, supplier, shipping promise or creative needs a change before another test.
StopDemand is thin or only a spike, margin works only on optimistic assumptions, or compliance, IP, quality or delivery cannot be controlled.

Write the decision down with the date and the evidence. Product research goes stale. A pass from last quarter is a reason to re-check, not a lifetime license.

Frequently asked questions

How do I validate a product idea without spending much money?

Do the desk work first: search trends, marketplace review activity, ad libraries and a conservative landed-cost sheet. That costs time, not ad budget. The first necessary expense is usually samples, because photos cannot validate quality or transit time. Only then run a small, pre-capped ad test.

Is a viral TikTok enough to validate a product?

No. A viral video is a discovery signal. Validation still needs demand outside that clip, workable margins, and a product you have actually received. See TikTok made me buy it product research.

How long should a validation test run?

Long enough to hit the stop or success rule you wrote down, and no longer. The right length depends on your budget, click volume and sales cycle. The important part is that the cap and the CPA target exist before the first ad runs.

What if demand looks good but the margin is thin?

That is a revise or a stop, not a go. You can try a different supplier, a bundle, a higher-value variant, or a market with a different price band. You should not launch on the hope that “scale will fix it.”

Can I use MarketScope as the only validation step?

No. It is a structured read of demand, competition, saturation and opportunity from the sources that are live on the report. It does not replace samples, cost calculation, compliance checks or a controlled test.
