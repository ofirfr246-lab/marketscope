Price bands: finding the gap nobody is competing in

Why the most crowded price is rarely the best place to compete, and how to read range, median and under-served bands — with a real MarketScope shopping example.

What a price band actually is

A price range tells you the cheapest and most expensive listing you can see. A price band is more useful: it is the cluster of offers around a similar price, where most of the market actually sits. Two products can share the same wide range and still have very different bands — one crowded at a single point, another spread out, with quieter stretches in between.

In product research, the question is not only “what do people charge?” It is “where are the listings bunched, where is the median, and is there a band where you could reasonably compete?” That is the work of reading price bands, and it should happen before you lock a retail price or spend on ads.

This is a companion to our guide on how to find winning products before you spend on ads. Demand and competition tell you whether a market is worth a closer look. Price bands tell you where in that market an offer might fit.

Why the most crowded price is rarely the easiest place to compete

Sellers often copy the price they see most often. That is understandable: it looks like “the market price.” It is also where comparison is fiercest. If many stores already sit on the same number, a new listing at that number has to win on something else — shipping, creative, reviews, or brand — none of which a price chart can promise.

A quieter band, just below or just above the median, can be a clearer place to test. Customers who were choosing between near-identical offers at the crowded point may notice a slightly sharper or slightly more premium price, if the offer still makes sense. Or they may not. The research job is to see the cluster and the quieter stretch, then judge them. It is not to assume that every empty-looking band is a hidden jackpot.

What to record when you look at prices

For a named product in a named market, write down at least:

  • The visible range — lowest and highest listing prices in the set you are actually using.
  • The median — a better anchor than the average, which a few expensive listings can pull up.
  • Where listings cluster — the crowded point or points, not just the ends of the range.
  • A candidate band you might test, and a one-line reason (for example, “just below the median” or “above a tight cluster”).

Do this in one market at a time. A US shopping snapshot and a UK one are not interchangeable. And use a current set of listings. Prices move; a band from last season is not a plan.

A gap is not automatically profit

An under-served band can be empty for a good reason. The price may be too low to cover shipping and returns. It may be too high for what customers will pay for an unproven store. It may sit in a quality gap: cheaper listings look flimsy, dearer ones include a brand or a bundle you cannot match yet.

Treat a gap as a hypothesis. It says: “fewer visible offers sit here.” It does not say: “this price will convert,” and it does not say: “your margin works.” Landed cost, delivery time and return risk still have to be checked with a sample and a calculator. MarketScope scores also do not include your costs. They describe the market you would be entering, not your P&L.

A real example, not a tidy success story

The public MarketScope demo is a real report, not a mock-up: a magnetic phone mount in the United States, built from live Google Shopping and Google Search data collected on 12 November 2026. The shopping set in that run showed:

  • A price range of $6.68–$69.
  • A median price of $19.99.
  • A recommended selling band of $14.99–$19.99, described as at or just below the median.
  • 65 Google Shopping listings from 29 sellers. No seller in the visible set held more than 12% of listings (Home Depot). The top three sellers together held 34% of listings.

That recommended band is a real output of that report. It is also not the end of the story. In the same run, demand scored 76/100 — about 8,100 monthly searches, with a roughly stable 12-month trend — but competition scored 90/100. Saturation was 64/100. Opportunity came out at 48/100, and the verdict was UNDERPERFORM. The advisor summary put the tension plainly: high competition versus solid demand.

That is the honest use of price bands. You can have a sensible place to sit on the price line and still be looking at a hard market. A band recommendation does not override a weak opportunity score. How to use a band in a test

If you decide the market is still worth a small test, use the band as a starting range, not as a promise:

  • Check that the candidate price still works after shipping, fees and a realistic return rate.
  • Keep the first test small and capped, with a stop rule, as in the winning-products framework.
  • If the band is just below the median, be honest about why you can sit there — a simpler offer, a tighter bundle, or a lower cost — rather than discounting into a hole.
  • If the band is above the median, be honest about what would justify it. A higher price without a clearer offer is just a hope.

And if the rest of the report is already an UNDERPERFORM or AVOID, a tidy-looking band is not a reason to ignore that. Price is one lens. Demand, competition and saturation are the others.
